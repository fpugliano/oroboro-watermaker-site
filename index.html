ORB-2521 at 30 Hz
Compact vessel fits tight lockers. 45 min run → ~26 L. Net battery draw: ~37 Ah. Run while motoring or right after engine stop.
Why the compact ORB-2521 and not the full ORB-2540?
Space, not output. The ORB-2521 uses a 21-inch pressure vessel — roughly half the length of the full model. On a 35–37ft boat the locker space is tight and every centimetre counts. Output is lower (~26 L per 45-minute run at 30Hz), but for two people being water-smart that is more than enough.
Does the bank survive the run?
With 100 Ah usable, one run draws ~37 Ah net (panel charges during the run). That leaves 63 Ah in reserve — well above the 50% limit if the bank was reasonably full. On a 35–37ft boat you motor more often than bigger boats anyway. Run the watermaker while the engine is running and it costs you nothing from the bank.
What about the inverter?
The VFD needs a pure sine wave inverter rated 2,000W minimum continuous. If you have a 1,000W or 1,500W unit — or a modified sine wave unit — replace it first. This is not optional. A 2,000W pure sine wave inverter costs €150–250 and is a worthwhile upgrade regardless of the watermaker.
Is 26 litres enough for two people?
13 litres per person per day is plenty for drinking, cooking, and quick rinses — especially on a boat where you are in the sea all day. Run it every other day and you have a comfortable buffer. At marinas, top up tanks for free and don't run it at all.

ORB-2540 at 30 Hz
1.5h midday run → ~52 L. Net battery draw: ~58 Ah, leaving 142 Ah in reserve. Engine top-up every 2–3 anchor nights. Very manageable.
What does one watermaker run actually cost?
At 30Hz the system draws ~700W through the inverter. Running 1.5 hours to produce ~52 litres costs ~1,050 Wh total. With 210W of solar charging simultaneously (~315 Wh recovered during the run), the net battery draw is ~735 Wh — about 61 Ah at 12V. That leaves 139 Ah in reserve. Comfortable, well above the 50% DoD limit.
Can 300W rigid solar cover it — with the fridge running all day?
Not entirely. The daily budget without the watermaker is ~1,250 Wh. Solar covers ~1,050 Wh, leaving a ~200 Wh deficit per day. Add the watermaker run and that deficit grows. In practice: one engine charging session every 2–3 anchor nights (you are probably motoring between islands anyway) balances the bank. It is very cheap water — not free water.
What is the trick with the fridge?
Pre-cool the fridge for an hour before the run, then switch the compressor off for the 90-minute watermaking window. The thermal mass of a well-insulated fridge holds temperature easily for that long. Net battery draw drops by 50–80W, the inverter is under less stress, and you recover 75–120 Wh over the run. It is the single most effective power management move on a hot Med day.
What if they switch to rigid solar?
Rigid panels at optimal angle run at 65–75% of nominal vs. ~50% for flexible flat-mounted. Upgrading to 400W rigid (same cost as 600W flexible) yields ~280W realistic — enough to nearly offset the watermaker run. Add a wind generator and the bank could stay positive without any engine charging on good days.

Do they ever need to run the engine just for the watermaker?
Almost never. They are already motoring between anchorages every few days — the engine charges the bank, the alternator runs at full output, and they can run the watermaker simultaneously at no additional cost. On anchor days with good sun, the 300 Ah usable bank handles the ~70 Ah watermaker draw and still has 230 Ah in reserve by the time they go to sleep. Engine charging happens once a week at most.
Can they dial the speed up on sunny days?
Yes — and this is where variable speed pays off. At 45Hz the system draws ~900W and produces ~50 L/h. On a good solar day their panels put back ~280W, leaving ~620W net draw. Running for 1.5 hours produces 75 litres — a full day's supply for two people, done before lunch. On cloudy days they drop to 30Hz, run longer, and draw less per litre.
What about hot water?
Their calorifier (engine heat exchanger) gives them free hot water on passage days. At anchor, a 230V element in the calorifier — powered via the Victron MultiPlus on a sunny afternoon — heats 20 litres in about 30 minutes at 800W. 400 Wh from solar surplus: hot shower, zero fuel. This is the liveaboard advantage.
What changes with lithium?
A 300 Ah LiFePO4 bank (same weight as 150 Ah AGM) gives them the same usable energy as their 600 Ah AGM — but 100% of it is available. The watermaker run uses 22% of the bank at 30Hz instead of 47%. Solar fills it back up comfortably on most Med days. Some liveaboards report running the watermaker every day on sun alone after the lithium upgrade.

Is a catamaran really the only setup where solar covers everything?
Almost. The arch on a 42–48ft cat holds 600–800W of rigid panels with a clean unobstructed sky view — no boom shadow, no sail shadow in the usual positions. At 65% realistic efficiency, 700W nominal yields ~455W. Over a 5-hour Med summer day: ~2,275 Wh. Total daily loads are ~1,580 Wh. That is a 695 Wh surplus — before the watermaker even runs. The watermaker run (1,400 Wh at 60Hz for 1 hour) temporarily dips the bank, but the surplus from the rest of the day fills it back. Engine optional.
100 litres for 6 people — how long does it take?
At 60Hz the system produces ~65–70 L/h. Run it for 1.5 hours and you get ~100 litres — about 16 litres per person. More than enough for cooking, drinking, and a proper rinse after snorkelling. Start the run at midday with the bank full from the morning sun, and the afternoon panels refill what the run used by sunset.
What about the AC?
The ORB-2540 draws 1,400W at 60Hz. Air conditioning on a cat draws 2,000–3,000W. Running both from the same inverter simultaneously is possible in theory — the inverter handles 3,000W — but you are asking 5,000W+ from the battery bank in one go. Better: run the watermaker at midday with no AC, then run AC in the evening when the bank is full and you are plugged into shore power or the engines are running.
Do they still need to run the engines?
Only to move between anchorages. Charging the bank from solar is sufficient for all loads including daily watermaking on sunny Med days. The alternators (2× 80A) charge the bank fast when motoring anyway. A 3-hour passage between islands typically tops everything up. Engine charging for battery purposes only: perhaps once every 7–10 days in prolonged bad weather.

The parts

Off-the-shelf. Industrial grade. Global stock.

Every component is a standard catalogue part from an established manufacturer. Nothing proprietary. Nothing that requires a specialist to source or replace.

Motor
Industrial 1.1kW three-phase motor. Runs at full rated speed via the VFD, driven by your boat's 230V inverter.
Variable Frequency Drive
Adjustable frequency drive — single-phase 230V in, three-phase 60Hz out. Soft start. Adjustable 30–60Hz for solar-only running.
High Pressure Pump
Italian ceramic triplex plunger pump. Water-lubricated, no oil, no maintenance schedule.
Membrane Vessel
FRP 2540 format — 1,000 PSI rated. Holds any standard 2.5" × 40" seawater RO membrane element.
HP Needle Valve
SS316L high-pressure needle valve, 344 bar rated, 3/8" BSPP ports. Controls system pressure and output flow.
Pressure Gauge
Glycerine-filled 0–160 bar gauge. Reads operating pressure in real time — the primary instrument for setting and monitoring the system.
Flow Meter
Inline on permeate outlet. Reads L/min in real time. Tracks membrane health and confirms rated output.
TDS Meter
Hand-held digital probe. Measures permeate salinity in ppm. Target: under 500 ppm from open ocean seawater.
Boost Pump
12V DC self-priming pump. Pulls seawater through the pre-filters and feeds the HP pump inlet at the right pressure.
Pre-Filter Housings
Big Blue 10" × 4.5". Two stage: 20µm then 5µm wound polypropylene. Protects the HP pump and membrane.

The name

Oroboro.
The eternal cycle.

ουρόβοροςThe ouroboros is an ancient Greek symbol — a serpent eating its own tail, forming a perfect circle. It represents the eternal cycle: self-renewal, endless return, the thing that feeds itself forever.

We chose it because a watermaker is the closest thing to that eternal cycle that exists aboard a boat. The sea is infinite. Our system draws from it as long as you sail. The water you make eventually returns to the sea. The cycle closes, and begins again.

It is also the simplest expression of what independence at sea actually means: not needing the shore for something as fundamental as drinking water. The serpent feeds itself. So do you.
