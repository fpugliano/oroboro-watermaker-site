The quay

You know the queue on the quay
when the meltemi blows.

Eight boats, one hose, a man with a clipboard. You pay for water that tastes of the pipe it came through, and you plan your week around it. Meanwhile you are floating on the largest reservoir on Earth.

Watermakers were supposed to fix this. Instead they arrived with a generator, an inverter the size of a suitcase, a panel of gauges and valves, and a maintenance schedule only an engineer could love. Most boats in the Mediterranean still don't have one. We think we know why.

Who builds it

Built by engineers. Not in a shed.

Most "affordable" watermakers are a pressure-washer pump, a length of hose and a brave weekend. They work, until the day they don't, usually somewhere without a chandlery.

The next Oroboro is designed by a team of aerospace and fluid-dynamics engineers, the people trained to build systems that must run for years with nobody around to fix them. Every part has a tolerance. Every seal has a rated life. Every failure has a plan.

Twelve volts

Designed around the power you already have.

Every boat has a battery bank. Very few have a three-kilowatt inverter or a generator that someone wants to run at anchor. So we started there, and we refused to move.

The next Oroboro runs on twelve volts, straight from the bank, at a draw you can sustain on solar. Not a smaller version of a big machine. A different machine.

Closed system

One button. Nothing to learn.

No pressure gauge to read. No needle valve to feather. No flush procedure to remember before you leave the boat for a month.

The machine knows when it last ran, rinses itself on schedule, and tells you one thing: the water is good, or it isn't. Everything else happens inside a sealed composite shell you will never need to open.

Installation

No haul-out. No new holes.

Your boat already has the openings a watermaker needs. The next Oroboro is designed to share them, so a weekend and a few hose clamps is the whole job.

Light enough to carry aboard in one hand. Compact enough to live in a locker you had forgotten about. If you can fit a cockpit cushion, you can fit this.

A boat belongs out there

When you can make your own water, the only thing between you and anywhere is the weather.

Space engineering

Built the way you build things
that cannot be fixed in orbit.

A spacecraft doesn't get a service call. So its designers obsess over three things: fewer parts, lighter materials, and systems that manage themselves. We gave this project to aerospace engineers and asked them to think the same way about a machine that lives in a bilge.

Composite instead of steel. Sealed instead of serviceable. Automatic instead of adjustable. Fewer things that can go wrong, because there are fewer things.
